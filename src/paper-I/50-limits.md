# Limits of the evidence

The evidence offered for the theses is of three kinds, and its weight differs.

*Proved statements.* Some passages cited above are theorems with proofs in the source: the vanishing of second
cohomology on graphs [@S02, §9.1.7], the counterexample to coverwise transport [@S02, Proposition 2.4.24], the
conditional bound on the index of the pole-removed Weil form [@S03, Corollary 46.11]. These are as reliable as their
proofs, which have not been checked by others. They support the theses only indirectly: they show that the
mathematics the theses rely on can be stated and, in places, proved.

*Specifications.* Most of the architecture is specified and not built. The chapters of the third volume of [@S01] record their implementation status as “specification only” [@S01, Vol. III, p. 8218], and [@S02] reports that no row of its claim ledger exceeds the lowest
licence grade [@S02, Proposition 1.2.3]. Theses 3, 4, 5 and 7 are therefore theses about a design. They could be wrong
in a way the texts cannot show: a structured carrier may be too slow, an obstruction too expensive to compute, a
capability model too restrictive to use. The problems attached to these theses are designed to find out.

*Records of method.* The five research subjects show what the method of Theses 8, 10 and 12 produces when it is carried
through: ledgers, obstructions, conditional chains, falsification tests. They do not show that the method is better
than another, because no comparison has been made. A reader may also observe that the records were written by the
author of the method, with the assistance of AI tools, and that a method assessed by its own records has not been
assessed. The theses are offered with that limit stated.

Three further limits should be named. First, the theses were selected by the author of the works from those works; a
reader starting from the same material might select differently. Second, the seven works are first drafts, and the
literature check carried out for *The 57 Grothendieckian Problems* found statements in them that are already superseded — for
example, a construction the source describes as unknown has since been published [@AMP2026]; other errors of this kind
are likely. Third, several theses are partly normative. Thesis 7, that humans authorise publication, is a proposal
about how mathematical work should be governed, and evidence can show what it costs but not settle whether it is
right.

The theses would be weakened in an identifiable way by any of the following: a reasoning system without world records,
status types or obstruction reports that matches one with them on the tasks of *The 57 Grothendieckian Problems*; an analysis
showing that the separation of fidelity from validity can be closed mechanically for the mathematics that matters;
or the five research subjects, examined by specialists, turning out to record nothing that the extended settings made
tractable. Each of these is a possible outcome, and the problems of *The 57 Grothendieckian Problems* are a way of reaching them.

# Conclusion

The twelve theses describe a way of combining mathematics with machine reasoning in which the context of a result is
an object, its status is part of its type, its failures are outputs, and its authority comes from outside the system
that produced it. They are drawn from seven works that apply the same discipline to themselves, at a length of 26,021
pages, and that report what they have not established alongside what they have.

The theses are not established by being stated. Several of them are claims about what will work, and *The 57 Grothendieckian Problems*
 turns them into fifty-seven problems with decision criteria. A thesis whose problems are solved in the direction
it predicts gains support; a thesis whose problems are solved against it should be revised or withdrawn, and the
record of that should be kept, as the theses themselves require.
